## The Wire-and-Moving-Charge Example

This is the classic illustration (it's essentially Einstein's own thought experiment, cleaned up by Purcell and others).

Picture a long, neutral, current-carrying wire — equal densities of positive ions (fixed in the wire lattice) and electrons (drifting to create current). A test charge $q$ moves parallel to the wire at velocity $v$, alongside the drifting electrons.

**In the wire's rest frame:** the wire is electrically neutral (equal charge density of + and -), so there's no electric field. The moving test charge feels a pure magnetic force $F = qv \times B$, from the magnetic field generated by the current.

**In the test charge's rest frame:** now the positive ions are moving backward and the electrons are (nearly) stationary. Length contraction applies *differently* to the two charge populations, because they have different velocities in this new frame. The positive-ion lattice's spacing contracts by a different factor than the electron spacing — so the two charge densities no longer exactly cancel. The wire looks charged. That residual charge density produces a genuine electric field, which pushes on the (now stationary) test charge with a pure electric force.

Same physical effect (the test charge gets deflected toward or away from the wire), but attributed to a magnetic force in one frame and an electric force in the other. Neither observer is wrong — they're just decomposing the same electromagnetic field tensor differently. That's the whole point: E and B aren't separate things that happen to interact; they're frame-dependent projections of one object.

## The Math: How E and B Mix

**Field transformation** (boost with velocity $v$ along $x$, $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}$):

$$E_x' = E_x \qquad B_x' = B_x$$
$$E_y' = \gamma(E_y - vB_z) \qquad B_y' = \gamma\left(B_y + \frac{v}{c^2}E_z\right)$$
$$E_z' = \gamma(E_z + vB_y) \qquad B_z' = \gamma\left(B_z - \frac{v}{c^2}E_y\right)$$

Components along the boost don't change; the transverse components mix.

**The field tensor** packages both fields into one antisymmetric 4×4 object:

$$F^{\mu\nu} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -E_x/c & -E_y/c & -E_z/c \\ E_x/c & 0 & -B_z & B_y \\ E_y/c & B_z & 0 & -B_x \\ E_z/c & -B_y & B_x & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

It transforms as a rank-2 tensor, $F'^{\mu\nu} = \Lambda^\mu_{\ \alpha}\Lambda^\nu_{\ \beta}F^{\alpha\beta}$, and the four Maxwell equations collapse into two:

$$\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu \qquad \partial_{[\lambda}F_{\mu\nu]} = 0$$

Two Lorentz invariants survive any boost — quantities every observer agrees on regardless of frame:

$$E^2 - c^2B^2 = \text{invariant}, \qquad \vec{E}\cdot\vec{B} = \text{invariant}$$

These are useful sanity checks: they're why, e.g., a field that's purely magnetic in one frame ($E=0$) can never become purely electric in another (since $\vec E \cdot \vec B=0$ is preserved, but $E^2-c^2B^2$ would have to flip sign, which the invariant forbids unless $B=0$ too).

# About electon drift being laughably slow

Good instinct, but let's separate two different smallnesses that easily get conflated.

**Drift velocity — yes, it's absurdly slow**

For ordinary current in copper wire, the electron drift speed is typically **fractions of a millimeter per second to a few mm/s** — not a fraction of $c$ in any dramatic sense, but tiny in absolute terms.

Quick calculation for 12 AWG copper wire (cross-section $A \approx 3.31\times10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$) carrying 10 A:

$$v_d = \frac{I}{n_e A e}$$

Copper's free-electron density is $n_e \approx 8.5\times10^{28}\,\text{m}^{-3}$, so:

$$v_d = \frac{10}{(8.5\times10^{28})(3.31\times10^{-6})(1.6\times10^{-19})} \approx 2.2\times10^{-4}\ \text{m/s} \approx 0.22\ \text{mm/s}$$

That's the famous "electrons drift slower than a snail" fact — even though the *signal* (the field disturbance) propagates through the wire at a large fraction of $c$.

In units of $c$: $\beta = v_d/c \approx 7\times10^{-13}$. The relativistic correction $\gamma - 1 \approx \beta^2/2 \approx 2.7\times10^{-25}$. That's not just small — it's absurdly, laughably small.

**So does that mean the force needs to be "extremely high"? No — the resolution is different.**

You're right that the *fractional* relativistic effect per electron is minuscule. But the reason ordinary wires still produce normal, everyday-strength magnetic forces isn't that anything about the force itself is boosted — it's that **the charge density in a wire is enormous**. For that same wire:

$$\lambda = n_e A e \approx 4.5\times10^{4}\ \text{C/m}$$

That's the magnitude of positive (ion) and negative (electron) charge per meter of wire — tens of thousands of coulombs per meter, sitting there almost perfectly cancelled. The tiny fractional imbalance from length contraction gets multiplied by this enormous number:

$$\Delta\lambda \sim \lambda \cdot (\gamma-1) \sim (4.5\times10^4)(2.7\times10^{-25}) \sim 10^{-20}\ \text{C/m}$$

That net charge, though minuscule, produces exactly the field strength you'd compute directly from $B = \mu_0 I / 2\pi r$ and $F = qv\times B$ — because that's what a self-consistent theory has to give.

This is sometimes called the **"conspiracy of large numbers"** (the term Purcell uses): a fantastically tiny relativistic correction times a fantastically large charge density equals an everyday-sized force. Magnetism is a relativistic effect that's normally hidden in plain sight precisely because both numbers involved are so extreme in opposite directions that they land back at "ordinary."
